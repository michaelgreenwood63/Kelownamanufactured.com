Park Redevelopment & Closure Risk

What's actually legally required if a park closes, and why that answer is very different depending on whose land it's on.

The short version: BC has real, legislated compensation requirements if a provincial-jurisdiction park closes — but there's no equivalent framework confirmed for Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7, and it's not clear WFN's own law mirrors BC's protections. This is one of the more consequential differences between the jurisdictions we cover, and it's worth understanding before you buy into a park with any real redevelopment pressure on it.

What BC law actually requires

Under BC's Manufactured Home Park Tenancy Act, a park owner who wants to close the park to change its land use generally needs the necessary permits and approvals in place first, then must give tenants 12 months' written notice. On or before the effective date, the owner is required to pay each tenant a minimum of $20,000 in compensation — more if the home can't be relocated and its assessed value exceeds that amount, in which case the tenant isn't responsible for disposal costs either. If an owner gives closure notice but doesn't actually follow through, they owe tenants 12 months' pad rental or $5,000, whichever is greater. These figures were set in 2018 — confirm the current amounts with BC's Residential Tenancy Branch before relying on them, since they can be updated.

Why this doesn't automatically apply on reserve land

Provincial legislation generally doesn't apply on reserve land — that's the same principle behind why BC's Manufactured Home Park Tenancy Act doesn't govern Westbank First Nation parks day-to-day (see our jurisdiction guide). The same logic applies to closure compensation: BC's $20,000-plus requirement is provincial law, and we have no confirmation it extends to WFN or Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7 land. WFN may have its own comparable provision within its Residential Premises Law — we haven't independently confirmed this — and for Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7, there's no dedicated framework at all. This is a genuinely important thing to ask about directly, not assume.

Real examples from our own directory

Two parks we track illustrate the range of this risk:

  • Central MHP (3535 Casorso Road, Lower Mission) sits on regular BC land and has been owned by a development company since 2020. A 2026 listing noted no imminent redevelopment plans — but it's investor-owned, on BC land, which means the $20,000-plus compensation framework above would apply if that ever changed.
  • Meadowbrook Estates (720 Commonwealth Road, Lake Country) sits on Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7, and the headlease is stated to expire in December 2026 — a genuinely near-term date. One remark notes the park manager has indicated operations will continue, but that's not the same as a confirmed renewal, and the BC compensation framework likely doesn't apply here even if the lease isn't renewed.

How to assess the risk on any specific park

  • Who owns it. A long-standing family-run park operator and a recently-acquired investor/developer carry different levels of redevelopment pressure.
  • Remaining headlease term. Not just whether it's long, but whether there's any public indication of renewal plans — or lack of them.
  • Land value trajectory. A park sitting on increasingly desirable land near growth areas carries more long-term redevelopment pressure than one in a more stable location.
  • Which jurisdiction applies. On BC land, you have a legislated financial floor if the worst happens. On reserve land, confirm what protection actually exists — don't assume parity.

Common Questions

Park Redevelopment & Closure Risk — FAQ

What compensation is required if a BC manufactured home park closes?
Under BC's Manufactured Home Park Tenancy Act, a park owner closing the park to change its use generally must give 12 months' notice and pay tenants a minimum of $20,000 in compensation — more if the home can't be relocated and is worth more than that. A park owner who gives closure notice but doesn't follow through owes 12 months' pad rental or $5,000, whichever is greater. Confirm current figures with BC's Residential Tenancy Branch, since amounts can be updated.
Does the same compensation apply on Westbank First Nation or Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7 land?
We haven't found evidence that it does. BC's compensation requirements are provincial legislation, and provincial law generally doesn't apply on reserve land. WFN's own Residential Premises Law may or may not include comparable provisions — confirm directly rather than assuming BC's protections carry over. No equivalent framework exists for Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7.
How do I tell if a specific park is at real redevelopment risk?
Look at who owns the park (an investor/developer versus long-term family operators), how much time is left on the underlying headlease, whether there's been any public indication of development plans or rezoning applications, and the park's broader context — a park sitting on increasingly valuable land near a growing area carries more long-term pressure than one in a stable, less desirable location.
Is a park with a short remaining headlease automatically risky?
It's worth real scrutiny, not an automatic disqualifier. A lease with a few years left could be renewed, or the landowner could have no interest in changing the park's use — but you shouldn't assume that. Ask directly what's actually known about renewal plans before buying into a park with a near-term lease expiry.
