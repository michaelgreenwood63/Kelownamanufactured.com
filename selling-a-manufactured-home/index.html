Selling a Manufactured Home: How It Actually Works

You're not just selling a home — you're assigning your spot in the park to the buyer, and the park gets a say.

The short version: in a leasehold park, selling a manufactured home means assigning your site tenancy or lease to the buyer — the park operator has to consent. On BC-jurisdiction land, this runs through a standard provincial form (RTB-10) with a 10-day response window. On Westbank First Nation or Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7 land, the mechanism is different — park/management approval still matters, but it isn't the BC RTB-10 process. Either way, it's a real step to plan your timeline around.

The assignment of tenancy — the core mechanism

Because you don't own the land under a leasehold park, selling the home itself isn't enough — the buyer also needs the right to keep it on that specific pad, which means your tenancy agreement (or sublease, on reserve land) has to be assigned to them. That assignment requires the park operator's consent. This is the single biggest structural difference between selling a manufactured home in a park and selling a conventional house.

For BC provincial-jurisdiction parks: the RTB-10 process

Where BC's Manufactured Home Park Tenancy Act applies (see our jurisdiction guide to confirm which parks these are), the mechanism is form RTB-10, Request for Consent to Assign a Manufactured Home Site Tenancy Agreement. A few specifics worth knowing:

  • The park owner has 10 days from receiving the request to respond.
  • If you haven't received a response by the end of that 10th day, consent is conclusively deemed to have been given — the park can't simply sit on it indefinitely.
  • A park can only refuse consent for specific, prescribed reasons — things like the buyer's credit or references, an age-restriction mismatch, the buyer not intending to actually live in the home, missing information on the request, or rent arrears owed by the seller. It isn't an open-ended veto.

For WFN and Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7 parks: a different process

BC's Manufactured Home Park Tenancy Act — and the RTB-10 form specifically — is a provincial mechanism, and provincial legislation generally doesn't apply on reserve land. Westbank First Nation parks operate under WFN's own Residential Premises Law, and Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7 parks appear to run on individual lease/sublease terms under the Okanagan Indian Band's land administration, without the same standardized provincial form. In practice, you'll still generally need the park's consent to assign the tenancy to your buyer — but the specific paperwork, timeline, and grounds for refusal are whatever that park's own lease or the relevant First Nation's framework sets out, not RTB-10. We confirm the actual process for the specific park before you list.

What to have ready before you list

  • Your current pad rent and what it includes (water, sewer, garbage — this varies a lot by park)
  • The park's current pet, rental, and age-restriction rules, in writing if possible
  • Whatever you know about the remaining lease term
  • Any correspondence from the park about upcoming changes

Buyers in this market increasingly ask about land jurisdiction specifically — see our jurisdiction guide for the exact language to use. Having that answer ready up front, rather than making a buyer dig for it, keeps a sale moving.

Why timeline planning matters here: the assignment-consent step adds real time to a sale that a conventional house doesn't have — plan your closing date with that in mind, and confirm the specific park's actual process rather than assuming RTB-10's 10-day window applies everywhere.

This page provides general information only and is not legal advice. The RTB-10 process described here applies specifically to BC provincial jurisdiction; reserve-land parks follow a different framework that varies by community. Please consult a lawyer or notary, and the specific park operator, about your situation.

Common Questions

Selling a Manufactured Home — FAQ

Do I need the park's permission to sell my manufactured home?
Yes, effectively. Selling means assigning your site tenancy or lease to the buyer, and the park operator has to consent to that assignment. On BC-jurisdiction land this goes through form RTB-10; on reserve land it follows that park's own approval process instead.
How long does the park have to respond to an assignment request in BC?
Under the RTB-10 process, the park owner has 10 days from receiving the request to respond. If you haven't received a response by the end of the 10th day, consent is conclusively deemed to have been given.
Can a park just refuse to approve my buyer?
Not for any reason. Under BC's framework, a park can only withhold consent for specific, prescribed reasons — things like the buyer's credit or references, failing an age requirement in an age-restricted park, the buyer not intending to live in the home, insufficient information, or rent arrears owed by the seller. It can't be an arbitrary refusal.
Does the RTB-10 process apply on Westbank First Nation or Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7 land?
No — RTB-10 is a BC provincial Residential Tenancy Branch form, and BC's Manufactured Home Park Tenancy Act doesn't apply on reserve land. WFN and Duck Lake IR#7 parks have their own approval processes for a sale, generally still requiring park/management consent in substance, but not through the provincial RTB-10 mechanism.
What should I disclose to a buyer before selling?
Current pad rent, the park's actual current rules (pets, rentals, age restrictions), the remaining lease term if you know it, and anything in writing from the park about upcoming changes. Buyers increasingly ask about land jurisdiction too — having that answer ready speeds things up.
