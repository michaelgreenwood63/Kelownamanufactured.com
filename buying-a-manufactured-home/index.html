Buying a Manufactured Home: The Real Process

It isn't just "make an offer, get a mortgage." There's a park-approval step and an assignment process most buyers don't see coming.

The short version: buying a manufactured home in a leasehold park means buying the home and separately being approved to take over the pad tenancy. Financing works differently than a conventional house, park management has to approve you, and the sale only closes once they've consented to the assignment. None of this is unusual or a red flag — it's just a different process than most buyers expect.

Step one: figure out which jurisdiction you're dealing with

Before anything else, find out whether the park sits on Westbank First Nation land, Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7, regular BC provincial land, or — rarely — land you'd actually own outright. This single fact changes which lenders will even consider the property, which legal framework governs your tenancy, and how the approval process works. See our full land jurisdiction breakdown, or check the specific park in our directory.

Step two: get pre-approved before you fall for a listing

Financing a home on leased land is genuinely different from a conventional mortgage — see our full financing guide for why. The short version: lenders willing to finance a specific park can be limited, sometimes to one or two names, especially on reserve land. Knowing your financing is actually possible for a specific community before you get attached to a listing saves a lot of disappointment.

Step three: park approval — often the step buyers don't expect

Most parks require their own approval before a new owner can move in, separate from your mortgage approval. Based on the current listings we track across many parks in this market, a minimum credit score in the 700-730 range comes up repeatedly, sometimes alongside a reference check or an application fee. This isn't universal — some parks are more relaxed — but it's common enough that you shouldn't assume it won't apply to you. We confirm the specific park's requirements before you write an offer.

Step four: the assignment of tenancy

Once your offer is accepted, you're not just buying the structure — you're taking over the seller's right to keep that home on that specific pad, under whatever tenancy or lease framework governs that park. The park operator has to consent to this assignment before the sale can close. For BC-jurisdiction parks this generally follows the standard RTB-10 process (see our selling guide for the full mechanics); reserve-land parks follow their own park's process instead. Either way, it's a real step with a real timeline — build it into your closing date expectations.

Step five: confirm the park's actual current rules

Pet policies, rental restrictions, and age requirements vary enormously from one park to the next — even between parks on the same street. You inherit the current rules as the new owner, not whatever informal understanding the seller may have had. Check the specific park's listing in our directory, and confirm directly before you close, especially if pets or future rental plans matter to you.

Why work with someone who already knows this market: most of the friction in a manufactured home purchase shows up in steps most buyers don't anticipate — financing limitations, park approval, and the assignment process. We flag all of it up front, park by park, instead of letting you discover it after you're attached to a listing.

This page provides general information only and is not legal, financial, or tax advice. Every park and every lender's requirements can differ, and rules change. Please confirm the specific details for any property with the park operator, your lender, and a lawyer or notary before relying on them.

Common Questions

Buying a Manufactured Home — FAQ

Do I need to be pre-approved before I can even look at a manufactured home?
Strongly recommended. Financing a home on leased land works differently than a conventional mortgage, and which lenders will even consider a specific park varies a lot — sometimes down to a single approved lender for a whole community. Sorting this out first avoids falling for a home you can't actually finance.
What is park approval and why does it matter before I make an offer?
Most parks require the park operator or management company to approve a new resident before you can move in — commonly including a minimum credit score (often in the 700-730 range based on what we've seen across many parks) and sometimes a reference check. This is separate from mortgage approval, and a surprising number of deals stall here, not at financing.
What's the assignment of tenancy, and why does it matter to a buyer?
You're not just buying the home — you're also taking over the seller's right to keep it on that specific pad, governed by whatever tenancy or lease agreement already exists with the park. The park operator has to consent to that transfer. It's a real step with its own timeline, not paperwork you can skip.
Do park rules like pets and rentals carry over to me as the new owner?
Generally yes — you're bound by the park's current rules, not whatever the seller happened to negotiate years ago. Pet policies, rental restrictions, and age requirements vary enormously park to park, so it's worth confirming the current rules for the specific park, not assuming they match a different community you've seen.
Does it matter whether the park is on reserve land?
Yes, in several practical ways — which lenders will finance it, which legal framework governs the tenancy, and how the approval process works. We check this before you make an offer, not after.
