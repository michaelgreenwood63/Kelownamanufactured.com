Kelowna Manufactured / WFN Land vs. BC Land

Whose Land Is Your Park Actually On?

At least three different legal frameworks govern manufactured home parks in this market — and most buyers never think to ask which one applies to them.

The short version: a manufactured home park's rules — pad rent limits, how a sale actually works, even whether you can get a mortgage — depend entirely on who owns the land underneath it. In the Central Okanagan that's one of: Westbank First Nation, Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7 (Okanagan Indian Band), regular BC provincial/municipal land, or — rarely — land you'd actually own yourself. This page explains the difference and how to tell which applies to a specific park.

Reserve land: not one framework, but several

A large share of West Kelowna's manufactured home parks, and a growing cluster in Lake Country, sit on First Nations reserve land — leased long-term to a park operator, who in turn collects pad rent from homeowners. Reserve land is not governed by BC's provincial tenancy laws at all. Provincial legislation generally doesn't apply on reserve land — each First Nation's own legal framework governs instead, and that framework is not the same from one reserve to the next.

Westbank First Nation (West Kelowna)

Westbank First Nation operates under its own self-government constitution and has passed its own Residential Premises Law, which governs landlord-tenant matters on WFN land — including the roughly two dozen manufactured home parks we've mapped along the Boucherie Road corridor, Shannon Lake, and the Highway 97 South strip. WFN's framework covers similar ground to BC's provincial Act in places (notice periods, dispute processes) but is its own distinct body of law, not a copy of it.

WFN headleases run extremely long — many to the 2080s through 2150s — paid upfront by the park operator in some cases, or on an ongoing basis in others. The length of the headlease term doesn't necessarily tell you much about your own situation as a homeowner, since most residents hold a month-to-month pad tenancy regardless of how many decades remain on the underlying lease.

Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7 (Lake Country / Oyama / Winfield)

Separately, we've confirmed that at least six manufactured home communities — Deer Meadows Estates, Duck Lake Estates (also called Creek Run Developments), Belaire Estates, Holiday Park Resort, Winfield Mobile Home Park, and Meadowbrook Estates — sit on Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7, administered by the Okanagan Indian Band. This is a completely different First Nation and reserve from Westbank First Nation, even though both fall under the general umbrella of "reserve land."

We have not found evidence that the Okanagan Indian Band has passed its own dedicated residential tenancy law the way WFN has. Based on what's publicly available, land administration there appears to run through the Okanagan Indian Band's Lands Department under general Indian Act leasing provisions — meaning your protections as a homeowner likely depend more heavily on the specific terms of your individual lease or sublease document, rather than a standalone tenancy statute with its own built-in protections. This is worth confirming directly with the Okanagan Indian Band's Lands Department or a lawyer before treating it as settled — we're not aware of a public source that resolves this as definitively as WFN's own published law does for West Kelowna.

One practical note: at least one Duck Lake IR#7 park (Meadowbrook Estates) has a head lease expiring in December 2026 — a genuinely near-term date, unlike the WFN leases running decades further out. If you're looking at a home in any reserve-land park, the remaining lease term is something to verify directly, not assume.

A park can have mixed parcels

We've also found a handful of parks — Sierra's Westside, Nancee Way Village, and Belaire Estates among them — where most listings show the park as being on reserve land, but one specific unit's listing shows otherwise. That suggests these parks may not sit on a single uniform parcel of land; some lots could be reserve land and others not, within the same community. Where that's the case for a park we cover, we say so rather than picking one answer — and it's exactly the kind of detail worth confirming for the specific unit you're looking at, not just the park's general reputation.

Regular BC provincial or municipal land

Plenty of parks in this market sit on ordinary land — no reserve involved at all — and fall under BC's Manufactured Home Park Tenancy Act like any other pad-rental community in the province. Annual pad rent increases are capped (tied to a formula based on inflation plus certain local cost increases), and selling your home means assigning the site tenancy agreement to the buyer using the standard RTB-10 process, with the park required to respond to a request for consent within a set window.

Crystal Springs and Trail Park Mobile Villas in West Kelowna, and Ranch Park, McCulloch Heights, and Antler Beach Estates elsewhere in the region, are confirmed examples of parks on this kind of land — verified directly against MLS legal description data, not assumed.

You own the land outright

A small number of communities — Pinewoods Villa on Lenz Road in West Kelowna is the clearest example — are set up as bare-land strata. In these, you don't lease a pad at all; you own your own parcel of land, with a modest strata fee covering shared costs like roads. This is a fundamentally different, and generally more secure, ownership structure than any leasehold arrangement, reserve or provincial — no pad rent increases to track, no lease expiry to worry about, no assignment-consent process standing between you and a sale.

Why this matters before you make an offer: land tenure affects financing (some lenders won't touch certain reserve-land parks at all, or only specific ones will), what "selling" actually involves, and what happens decades from now when a lease term runs out. We check this before you fall in love with a listing, not after.

This page provides general information only. It is not legal advice, and jurisdiction can be genuinely ambiguous for a specific parcel. Please confirm the specific situation for any property with a lawyer or notary before relying on it.

Common Questions

Land Jurisdiction — FAQ

How do I find out whose land my manufactured home park is on?
Check the legal description on the title or listing sheet — it will name an Indian Reserve (e.g. "Tsinstikeptum Indian Reserve No. 9" for Westbank First Nation, or "Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7") if the park sits on reserve land. If it just shows a standard CLSR/plan number with no reserve name, it's likely regular provincial or municipal land. We've mapped this for every park we cover.
Does BC's Manufactured Home Park Tenancy Act apply on Westbank First Nation land?
No. WFN has its own self-government authority and has passed its own Residential Premises Law, which governs tenancies on WFN land instead of BC's provincial Act. The frameworks are similar in places but not identical, and WFN's own law controls.
Is Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7 the same as Westbank First Nation?
No — they're different reserves belonging to different First Nations. Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7 is administered by the Okanagan Indian Band, not Westbank First Nation. We haven't found evidence that the Okanagan Indian Band has passed its own dedicated residential tenancy law the way WFN has, which means protections there likely rely more heavily on the specific lease or sublease document and the general Indian Act leasing framework — worth confirming directly with the band's Lands Department or a lawyer before relying on any assumption.
Can one park have lots on both reserve and non-reserve land?
It happens. A small number of parks have most of their lots on reserve land, but a specific unit turns out to sit on a different kind of parcel entirely — the park's land isn't always one uniform tenure. When that's the case for a park we cover, we say so rather than picking one answer.
What about bare-land strata manufactured home communities?
A handful of Okanagan communities are set up as bare-land strata, where you actually own your parcel of land outright rather than leasing a pad. This is a fundamentally different — and generally more secure — ownership structure than any leasehold park, reserve or provincial.
