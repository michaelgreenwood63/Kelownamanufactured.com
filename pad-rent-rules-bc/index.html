Pad Rent Rules in BC

How much it can go up, how much notice you get, and what's actually included — for parks on regular provincial land.

The short version: on BC provincial-jurisdiction land, annual pad rent increases are capped by a formula set each year, landlords must give 3 full months' written notice, and disputes go through the Residential Tenancy Branch. None of this applies on Westbank First Nation or Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7 land — see our jurisdiction guide to confirm which framework applies to a specific park before relying on any of this.

How much can pad rent increase?

Under BC's Manufactured Home Park Tenancy Act, the allowable annual rent increase is set by a formula that changes each year. For increases taking effect in 2026, the formula is 2.3%, plus the proportionate increase in local government levies and regulated utility fees for common property. That second part means the actual increase a specific park can charge may run a bit above 2.3% depending on real cost increases the park operator faces — but it isn't an open-ended number, and a landlord can't just pick a figure. Because this formula is set annually, always confirm the current year's rate before relying on a specific number.

Notice requirements

A park operator must give 3 full months' written notice of a rent increase, using the proper notice form. Rent can only be increased once every 12 months for a given tenancy. An increase given with insufficient notice, or exceeding the allowable amount, isn't automatically valid just because it was delivered.

What pad rent typically includes — and doesn't

This varies a lot more than people expect. Across the parks we track, we've seen pad rent that includes water, sewer, and garbage collection bundled in, and we've seen pad rent that covers none of that, with garbage billed separately by a private contractor and water/sewer billed quarterly on top. Neither pattern is more "normal" than the other — it genuinely depends on the specific park, and it's one of the first things worth confirming before comparing two listings on price alone. Check our park directory for what we've confirmed per community.

If you think an increase is wrong

For parks on BC provincial jurisdiction, disputes over a rent increase — whether it followed the correct process, used the right amount, or was properly noticed — go through BC's Residential Tenancy Branch, the same body that handles standard residential tenancy disputes generally. This avenue is specific to BC-jurisdiction land; it doesn't apply to WFN or Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7 parks, which have their own frameworks.

Why this matters when comparing listings: a lower sticker price with pad rent that excludes utilities can end up costing more monthly than a higher pad rent that includes everything. We help you compare the real, all-in picture, not just the headline number.

This page provides general information about BC's Manufactured Home Park Tenancy Act as it applies to parks on regular provincial land, and is not legal advice. The specific annual rent-increase formula changes each year — confirm the current figure with the Residential Tenancy Branch or a lawyer before relying on it for a specific situation.

Common Questions

Pad Rent — FAQ

How much can pad rent increase per year in BC?
For rent increases taking effect in 2026, the allowable formula is 2.3% plus the proportionate increase in local government levies and regulated utility fees for common property. This is set annually and can change year to year, so always confirm the current figure before relying on it.
How much notice does a park have to give before a rent increase?
Three full months' written notice, using the proper form. A rent increase given with less notice, or exceeding the allowable amount, isn't automatically binding.
Does this apply to Westbank First Nation or Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7 parks?
No. These rules come from BC's provincial Manufactured Home Park Tenancy Act, which doesn't apply on reserve land. WFN parks follow WFN's own Residential Premises Law instead, and Duck Lake IR#7 parks appear to run on individual lease terms without a published, equivalent rent-cap formula that we've been able to confirm.
What does pad rent actually include?
It varies significantly by park. Some pad rents include water, sewer, and garbage; others bill these separately, sometimes to a private company rather than the park itself. We've seen real examples of both patterns across parks in this market — always confirm what's actually included for a specific park rather than assuming.
What can I do if I think a rent increase is wrong or unfair?
For BC-jurisdiction parks, disputes go through BC's Residential Tenancy Branch, the same body that handles standard residential tenancy disputes. They can review whether an increase followed the correct process and amount.
