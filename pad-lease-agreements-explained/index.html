Pad Lease & Site Tenancy Agreements

Most leasehold parks actually run on two separate leases stacked on top of each other — here's what each one does.

The short version: in almost every leasehold park we track, there are two distinct agreements. The headlease is between the landowner (Westbank First Nation, the Okanagan Indian Band, or a private landholder) and the park operator — these are the long terms you see in our park directory, often running 80 to 125 years. Your pad lease or site tenancy agreement is a separate, shorter document between you and the park operator, covering your specific lot. Almost every homeowner only ever sees and signs the second one.

Why the distinction matters

Because these are two separate documents, the headlease having decades left doesn't automatically tell you much about your own situation. We've reviewed actual WFN headleases running to 2145 that pass through to park operators with full freedom to assign, sublet, and manage the park — but your individual pad tenancy is still its own agreement, typically month-to-month, governed by whatever rules the park operator sets within the limits of the applicable jurisdiction (BC's Manufactured Home Park Tenancy Act, WFN's Residential Premises Law, or the less-defined framework on Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7 — see our jurisdiction guide).

What a typical pad lease actually covers

  • Pad rent amount and adjustment terms — how much, how often it can change, and under what process. On BC-jurisdiction land this is capped by provincial rules (see our pad rent rules guide); on reserve land, the limits depend on that jurisdiction's own framework and the document itself.
  • What's included — water, sewer, garbage collection, and road/common-area maintenance are sometimes bundled into pad rent and sometimes billed separately. There's real variation on this across our directory — never assume, confirm per park.
  • Park rules and bylaws — pets, rentals, parking, exterior modifications, and age restrictions are usually incorporated by reference rather than spelled out in full in the lease itself. Ask for the actual current rules document, not just a verbal summary.
  • Assignment / consent-to-sell clause — the mechanism that makes selling your home possible at all, since you're really assigning your tenancy to the buyer, not transferring land title. See our selling guide for how this works in practice.
  • Termination provisions — the grounds and process for the park ending your tenancy, separate from the broader question of the park itself closing (see our redevelopment and closure risk guide for that).

Dispute resolution depends on jurisdiction

If something goes wrong on BC-jurisdiction land, BC's Residential Tenancy Branch handles disputes under the Manufactured Home Park Tenancy Act. On Westbank First Nation land, disputes fall under WFN's own Residential Premises Law and governance structures instead. On Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7, we haven't found evidence of an equivalent dedicated dispute-resolution framework, which means more may come down to the specific wording of your individual agreement — all the more reason to read it carefully and have a lawyer review it before signing, rather than assuming a standard process applies.

Before you sign anything: ask for both documents if they exist — the headlease (to understand the park's long-term security) and your own pad lease (to understand your specific terms) — and have a lawyer or notary review the pad lease specifically, since that's the document that actually governs your day-to-day situation.

Common Questions

Pad Lease Agreements — FAQ

What's the difference between a headlease and a pad lease?
The headlease is the big, long-term agreement between the landowner (a First Nation, or a private landholder) and the park operator — these are the 80-to-125-year terms you see in our park directory. The pad lease, or site tenancy agreement, is the separate, much shorter document between you and the park operator, governing your specific pad. Most homeowners only ever see and sign the second one.
What's usually included in pad rent, and what isn't?
It varies park to park — some pad rents include water and sewer, others bill those separately, and some include garbage collection while others don't. Never assume; check the specific pad lease or ask the park directly what's included before you rely on an advertised pad rent figure.
Can a park change the rules in my pad lease after I move in?
Generally changes need to follow whatever process the applicable law requires — BC's Manufactured Home Park Tenancy Act has specific rules about this for provincial-land parks. On WFN or Duck Lake Indian Reserve No. 7 land, the process depends on that jurisdiction's own framework and the specific wording of your agreement. Read the actual document rather than assuming.
Does my pad lease end if the park's headlease ends?
It depends on the terms of both documents and which jurisdiction applies — this is exactly the kind of risk worth understanding before you buy, especially in a park with a shorter remaining headlease term. See our guide to park redevelopment and closure risk.
